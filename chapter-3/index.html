Intervals

Read the interval. Hear the interval. Put it into practice.

Intervals above F4

How do musicians measure the distance between notes?

Have you ever watched the show Hell’s Kitchen? When Gordon Ramsay isn’t putting hamburger buns on a chef’s ears and calling them an idiot sandwich, one of the tests he has for the chefs is the “taste it and make it” challenge. The chefs have 30 minutes to make a dish after tasting it just once. A great chef doesn't just say, "That tastes good." They can identify the acidity, the herbs, the cooking technique, and the balance of flavors. In the same way, a great musician doesn't simply hear a melody, they hear the intervals, rhythms, harmonies, and textures that created it.

One of the first major steps in that direction is to deeply internalize all the possible musical intervals. The good news is there aren’t that many intervals to learn. The bad news is that there are several variations of each interval, and some intervals can even sound the same while being spelled differently. These are called enharmonic equivalents, and you'll learn about them later. What is certain is once you learn your intervals you will never be able to hear the world the same way.

The two notes the train plays are the notes G and D and we measure the distance between them and call it a Perfect 4th. Once you learn your intervals you start hearing them everywhere. Once you master them you unlock a whole new world of musical understanding. If scales are the palettes composers choose from, intervals are the building blocks from which melodies and harmonies are constructed. Nearly everything you will study for the rest of this book - from scales and chords to voice leading and form - can ultimately be understood as relationships between intervals.

So what are intervals?

Intervals describe the distance between any two given notes. You have probably heard some of the different intervals spoken before as in the last paragraph when I mentioned a Perfect 4th and Perfect 5th. Notice there are two parts to an interval name, the descriptor (Perfect in this case) and the numeric interval (4th or 5th in this case). You are going to learn about where both of those parts in the name come from beginning with the numeric interval.

The number part of the interval is often referred to as the generic interval. Generic intervals refer to how many lines or spaces two notes are away from each other. We count each line and space as a separate number so for example if we choose the notes F and A in treble clef, as in the picture below:

Notice the F is in the lowest space in the staff and the A is in the second space in the staff with the second line of the staff in between them. To find the generic interval formed by these two notes we simply count beginning with F as 1, then the line between them is 2 and finally A is 3. This means the generic interval between F and A is a 3rd. Very importantly, this also means that no matter what flats or sharps are put on those two pitches, if there is an F and an A the interval is a 3rd. F# and Ab? 3rd. Fb and A# Still a 3rd. F double-flat and A double-sharp, still a 3rd. By definition any kind of F with any kind of A above it is going to create a 3rd. Now that 3rd can be major or minor, it can be diminished or augmented but it will always be a 3rd.

Moving on to the descriptor part of an interval name (i.e; the “Perfect” in Perfect 4th), there are five words you will usually see as the descriptor names: Perfect, Major, Minor, Augmented and Diminished. As you get into more advanced situations you may also come across doubly augmented and doubly diminished but those are more rare.

The first thing you should understand about these descriptor names is that Perfect Intervals are never Major or Minor and vice versa. This is a confusing thing to learn at first so try to remember that we only use the term Perfect for 1sts, 4ths, 5ths and 8ths. Conversely we never use the term Major or Minor for those intervals. 2nds, 3rds, 6ths and 7ths, are Major or Minor and never Perfect.

Why are interval names separated this way?

Early music theorists recognized that unisons, fourths, fifths, and octaves behaved differently from the other intervals. Rather than calling them major or minor, they reserved the word "perfect" for these especially stable intervals in the music of the time.

Ultimately the terminology is used to describe the possible distances between notes. Pick any note you want and then think the next closest note up from that note. Then the next closest and the next and so on. We have names for each one of those intervals and it is easiest to simply learn the names of the intervals within an octave and internalize them deeply. Mastering these intervals will be a powerful skill that will open up new worlds of musical understanding for you but it is also a difficult step in your musical journey. It will take lots of repetition and practice.

It is important for you to understand that there are two separate but related skills you need to master with intervals: the first is the ability to visually identify intervals and the second is the ability to aurally identify intervals and sing them. You will need to practice each skill independently.

Different intervals shape musical gravity. A perfect fifth feels open and stable, while a minor second often feels tense and compressed. Learning intervals is really learning the basic vocabulary of musical gravity.

Hearing Intervals

When musicians identify an interval by ear, they are usually not measuring it mathematically. Instead, they recognize its sound through repeated exposure. Over time, each interval develops its own unique musical character. Eventually, hearing a perfect fifth or a minor second becomes as immediate as recognizing the voice of a close friend.

Don't worry if these intervals don't become second nature immediately. Every professional musician once had to stop and think about whether they were hearing a major sixth or a minor seventh. Like learning a new language, fluency comes through repeated exposure and use.

Enharmonic Intervals

The last thing we need to mention as it relates to intervals is the confusing fact that intervals which sound the same can be spelled differently. For example if we take the interval of the tri-tone which is formed by two notes 6 half steps apart, if we think of that interval as being a kind of 5th we can call it a Diminished 5th and if we think of it as a 4th we can call it an Augmented 4th. Which one we call it depends on how it is spelled. If for example the notes C and F# are notated then we would call it an augmented 4th but if the notes C and Gb are notated we would call it a Diminished 5th. In either case the sound would be identical.

Why spell it differently?

We spell it differently based on the key we are in, something you will learn about in the next chapter. One way to think about this is analogously to spelling words in the English language like “Color”. If you were living in England the correct spelling of the word would be “Colour” whereas in the United States we spell it “Color”. The words sound the same and have the same meaning but the spelling changes based on where you are when you are writing it. We spell different intervals differently based on which key we are in when we are writing it and it is important you learn to spell them correctly.
