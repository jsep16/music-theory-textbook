Music Theory · Chapter 9

Roman Numerals

Hear the chords. Name their relationship to the key.

How does music tell us where it is going and when it has arrived?

Imagine listening to a familiar melody that stops just before its end. You may not be able to explain exactly what is missing, but the music feels unfinished and seems to want to continue. Restore the final note and chord, and the same melody can suddenly feel complete. Sometimes the difference is just one chord.

This is musical gravity, an idea we have developed throughout the book. Some notes and chords feel stable; others create tension or release it. We have learned to build triads and seventh chords and to recognize their inversions. Now we need to ask what happens when we put those chords together.

Just as words combine into sentences, chords combine into musical phrases. Their order can create expectation, motion, tension, surprise, and resolution. These chord progressions become easier to understand when we can describe how each chord relates to the key. Roman numerals give us that vocabulary.

Each chord in this example uses only notes from B♭ major, yet their qualities differ: some are major, some minor, and one diminished. That pattern comes from the major scale’s whole steps and half steps. It is the same in every major key.

Building Chords from a Scale

A major scale contains seven different pitch classes. B♭ major uses B♭-C-D-E♭-F-G-A, returning to B♭ at the octave. Build a triad on each scale degree by stacking thirds and using only notes from that scale.

Starting on B♭ gives B♭-D-F, a B♭ major triad. Starting on C gives C-E♭-G, or C minor; starting on D gives D-F-A, or D minor. Continue through the scale to find all seven diatonic triads.

The Pattern of Diatonic Triads

Every major key produces the same pattern: major, minor, minor, major, major, minor, diminished. The table below shows the same process in C major, whose scale uses C-D-E-F-G-A-B.

Scale degrees 1, 4, and 5 produce major triads; 2, 3, and 6 produce minor triads; and 7 produces a diminished triad. Once you know the key, you know the qualities of its seven diatonic triads. You can learn one pattern rather than memorize a separate quality pattern for every major key.

What Does Diatonic Mean?

A diatonic chord uses notes from the prevailing diatonic collection without adding chromatic alterations. C major, D minor, G major, and A minor are all diatonic to C major because all their notes belong to the C major scale.

D major is not diatonic to C major because its F♯ lies outside that scale. We will study chromatic chords later; for now, chords built from the key’s basic collection give us a foundation for understanding tonal harmony.

Roman Numerals

Chord names identify particular pitches, but the names change when the key changes. The tonic chord is still built on scale degree 1, the dominant on 5, and the subdominant on 4. Roman numeral analysis describes these relationships independently of the key by identifying the scale degree on which each chord’s root is built.

In C major, C major is I, D minor is ii, E minor is iii, F major is IV, G major is V, A minor is vi, and B diminished is vii°. The numerals correspond to the roots’ scale degrees, while their capitalization helps show the triad qualities.

Uppercase and Lowercase Roman Numerals

Uppercase numerals indicate major triads, and lowercase numerals indicate minor triads. A small degree symbol, °, marks a diminished triad. In every major key, the resulting pattern is I-ii-iii-IV-V-vi-vii°.

The major-key pattern

I - ii - iii - IV - V - vi - vii°. Learn the capitalization and diminished symbol together; they identify the qualities as well as the scale degrees.

The Same Progression in Different Keys

Roman numerals let us describe a progression independently of its particular chord names. For example, I-IV-V-I uses different pitches in C major and G major, while preserving the same harmonic relationships.

Chord Identity and Harmonic Function

A Roman numeral identifies a chord’s root and quality relative to the key, but it does not explain everything the chord is doing. A vi chord might substitute for tonic, move toward a predominant chord, or provide the unexpected destination of a deceptive cadence. Its numeral stays the same while its musical role changes with context.

Knowing a chord’s notes is therefore different from understanding its function. Roman numerals give us a vocabulary for describing harmony; listening and context help us understand how that harmony behaves. Later work on progressions and cadences will develop this distinction further.

What About Minor Keys?

Minor keys are more flexible than major keys because scale degrees 6 and 7 can change with the melodic and harmonic context. Begin with A natural minor, A-B-C-D-E-F-G. Triads built from those notes give the following pattern:

The Leading Tone and the Major Dominant

In A minor, E minor contains E-G-B, and G lies a whole step below tonic A. To create a stronger leading-tone pull, composers often raise scale degree 7 from G to G♯. The dominant then becomes E-G♯-B, or E major: V rather than v.

Building a triad on the raised leading tone gives G♯-B-D, labeled vii°. Common-practice minor-key harmony therefore often uses i-ii°-III-iv-V-VI-vii°. The major dominant supports a strong V-i resolution, just as major keys commonly use V-I.

This is not a single rigid minor scale. The minor dominant v and the subtonic chord VII also occur, and other contexts offer additional possibilities. Raising scale degree 7 within the chord on degree 3 creates an augmented triad, III+. The key idea is that minor uses a flexible collection of pitches, especially on degrees 6 and 7.

Roman Numerals and Musical Gravity

Scales gave notes relationships to tonic, intervals described relationships between notes, and chords combined those intervals into harmonic structures. Roman numerals now describe those structures in relation to a tonal center. Tonic establishes home, a predominant often prepares the dominant, and the dominant pulls toward home. Cadences, which we will study in more detail later, organize that motion into phrases.

In C, G, and B♭ major, the progressions C-F-G-C, G-C-D-G, and B♭-E♭-F-B♭ all follow I-IV-V-I. They contain different notes but share the same underlying journey. Learning to hear those relationships helps you recognize patterns that connect a great deal of music, even when its key changes.

Summary

Roman numeral analysis describes chords according to their roots and qualities relative to a key. Major-key diatonic triads follow I-ii-iii-IV-V-vi-vii°. In minor, flexible scale degrees 6 and 7 allow more than one form of some chords; V and vii° are especially important in creating a strong pull toward tonic.
