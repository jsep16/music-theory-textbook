The first chord may sound bright, familiar, and settled; the second may sound darker or more reflective. The third may sound tense or unstable, as though it wants to move somewhere else. How can such small changes produce such different results?

Harmony is the way pitches combine and interact. When several notes sound together, we hear both the individual notes and the relationships between them. Those relationships can create stability, tension, color, and motion.

In earlier chapters, intervals described relationships between two notes. Now we will combine intervals to create chords and discover how their construction influences our experience of musical gravity.

From Intervals to Chords

An interval describes the relationship between two notes: a major third, a minor third, or a perfect fifth, for example. When we play C, E, and G together instead of just C and E, we combine several interval relationships into one larger harmonic structure. That structure is called a chord.

What Is Tertian Harmony?

The word tertian refers to thirds. Tertian harmony is built by stacking thirds: begin on C, move up a third to E, then move up another third to G. The result, C-E-G, is a triad. Adding another third produces a seventh chord; we will study those four-note chords in a later chapter.

Stacking thirds became a central organizing principle of Western common-practice tonal music and remains influential in classical music, jazz, popular music, and many other traditions. It is not the only way to organize harmony, and even Western music frequently moves beyond tertian structures. Understanding it nevertheless gives us a foundation for much of the music in this book.

What Is a Triad?

A triad contains three different notes that can be arranged as a stack of thirds. The chord members are called the root, third, and fifth because of their intervals above the root. In C-E-G, C is the root, E is the third, and G is the fifth.

These names remain the same even when we rearrange the notes. C remains the root of a C major triad whether C, E, or G is the lowest sounding note. This distinction will become important when we study inversions.

The Four Types of Triads

The four basic triad qualities are major, minor, augmented, and diminished. Each uses a particular pattern of two stacked thirds, so we need to understand only two intervals - the major third and minor third - and combine them in four ways.

Major Triads

A major triad combines a major third with a minor third. To build C major, start on C: a major third above C is E, and a minor third above E is G. The result is C-E-G.

Measured above the root, the chord contains a major third and a perfect fifth. Listen to the same notes sounded together as a chord and one after another as an arpeggio.

Try playing or singing major triads on several different roots. Although their pitches change, the interval relationships remain the same. Over time, learn to recognize this shared quality regardless of the starting note.

Minor Triads

A minor triad reverses the pattern: a minor third followed by a major third. From C, a minor third gives E♭; a major third above E♭ gives G. C minor is therefore C-E♭-G, containing a minor third and perfect fifth above the root.

Listen: C major compared with C minor

Download recording

C major and C minor share their root and fifth. Only E changes to E♭, yet that half-step change significantly alters the character of the harmony. It demonstrates the expressive power of intervals within a chord.

Augmented Triads

An augmented triad contains two major thirds. Start on C, move up a major third to E, and move up another major third to G♯. The resulting C-E-G♯ contains a major third and an augmented fifth above the root.

Listen: C major compared with C augmented

Download recording

Compared with C major, the fifth has risen by one half step. Without the usual perfect fifth, an augmented triad can feel uncertain or suspended. Composers use augmented triads for chromatic tension and smooth movement between harmonies; we will revisit them in chromatic harmony.

Diminished Triads

A diminished triad contains two minor thirds. Starting on C, a minor third gives E♭; another minor third gives G♭. C diminished is C-E♭-G♭, containing a minor third and diminished fifth above the root.

Listen: C minor compared with C diminished

Download recording

The fifth is one half step lower than the perfect fifth of C minor. C-G♭ is a diminished fifth, one spelling of the tritone you studied in Chapter 3. This interval helps explain the chord’s tension, and diminished triads will become especially important in leading-tone chords, dominant function, and cadences.

A Better Way to Think About Triads

Rather than memorizing dozens of chords individually, learn the relationships that create them. A major triad is an interval structure, and so is every other triad quality. Once you understand that structure, you can build it on any root: learn the relationship, and the individual examples become easier to understand.

How to Build Any Triad

First identify the root, then choose the chord quality and build the required intervals. The process stays the same on every root. Here are the four triads built on F:

Notice that F diminished uses C♭ rather than B natural. Those pitches sound the same on an equal-tempered piano, but C♭ is a diminished fifth above F. Correct spelling shows the structure: build thirds rather than choosing any piano keys that produce the desired sound.

Triads and Musical Gravity

Triads combine intervals into larger harmonic structures. Major and minor triads both contain a perfect fifth above the root and can sound relatively stable in many tonal contexts. Augmented and diminished triads alter that fifth, which can create a greater sense of instability or uncertainty.

A chord’s quality does not tell us everything about its function. A major triad can create tremendous tension as a dominant, while a minor triad can provide a satisfying sense of home as a tonic. Even a diminished triad can feel familiar when it appears where the listener expects it. Later, we will ask both what kind of chord we hear and what it is doing in the music.

Triad Inversions

When the root is the lowest note, a triad is in root position. C-E-G is a root-position C major triad. Rearrange those same notes as E-G-C, and the third is lowest: this is first inversion. With G-C-E, the fifth is lowest, giving second inversion.

All three positions contain the same pitch classes and have the same chord quality. They do not sound exactly alike, however: changing the bass affects the character and direction of the harmony.

Bass and Root Are Not the Same Thing

The root is the note the chord is named after; the bass is the lowest sounding note. In every inversion of C major, C remains the root. In root position C is also the bass; in first inversion the bass is E, and in second inversion it is G.

Inverting a chord changes its bass position without changing its identity. The bass plays a large role in harmonic movement, and a carefully chosen inversion can make a progression more elegant. This will matter when we study voice leading and chord progressions.

Inversion vs. Voicing

Inversion describes which chord member is in the bass. Voicing describes how the notes are distributed among registers or voices. For example, C4-E4-G4 and C4-G4-E5 are both root-position C major chords, but the second spreads the notes across a wider range.

A close voicing keeps the upper chord tones relatively near one another; an open voicing spreads them farther apart. Changing the spacing does not necessarily change the inversion. This distinction will become essential when we write in four voices.

Remember

Changing the voicing does not necessarily change the inversion. Changing the lowest chord member does.

Summary

Tertian harmony is based on stacking thirds. A triad has three distinct chord members - root, third, and fifth - and four basic qualities: major (M3 + m3), minor (m3 + M3), augmented (M3 + M3), and diminished (m3 + m3).

Each triad has three possible bass positions: root position, first inversion, and second inversion. Identify the chord’s root and quality, then determine which member is in the bass. Keep the root distinct from the bass, and the inversion distinct from the voicing.

Musical Challenge

Choose a short melody you know well: a folk song, hymn, popular melody, or a tune from a piece you are studying. Harmonize it using major and minor triads, then listen to how each chord supports the melody and shapes its tension or sense of home.

Next, keep the chord qualities the same but experiment with inversions. Change which chord members appear in the bass and listen to the resulting bass line. One inversion may connect more smoothly to the next chord, even when both choices are theoretically correct.

Music theory helps us understand why one choice may produce a more convincing result than another. You now have the basic vocabulary of triads; next we will explore how chords function, connect, and create the musical gravity that shapes tonal music.

Listen and reflect

Which inversion gives the smoothest bass line? Where does the harmony feel settled, and where does it seem to ask for movement? Explain one chord choice you would keep and one you would change.
