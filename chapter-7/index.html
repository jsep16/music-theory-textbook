Seventh Chords

Add a seventh. Hear the color. Follow the bass.

How can we add more color and tension to a triad?

A triad consists of three notes built in thirds: root, third, and fifth. Add another third above C-E-G, placing B above G, and the result is C-E-G-B. This seventh chord has four distinct chord members: root, third, fifth, and seventh. The fourth member is called the seventh because it lies a seventh above the root; in this example, C to B is a major seventh.

Five Common Seventh-Chord Qualities

The five types studied here are major seventh, dominant seventh (also called major-minor seventh), minor seventh, half-diminished seventh, and fully diminished seventh. Each uses the same process: build a triad, then add the appropriate seventh above its root. We are building on the intervals and triads you already understand.

The basic formula

Triad + seventh = seventh chord. The triad quality and the interval from root to seventh determine the seventh-chord quality.

Major Seventh Chords

A major seventh chord combines a major triad with a major seventh above the root. Start with C major, C-E-G, and add B, a major seventh above C. The result is C-E-G-B, or C major seventh. Common symbols include Cmaj7, CM7, and CΔ7.

The added B gives the chord a new color. A major seventh can create richness or gentle tension, depending on the context. The harmony is still rooted in C, but its sound is more complex than the original triad.

Dominant Seventh Chords

A dominant seventh chord combines a major triad with a minor seventh above the root. Starting with C-E-G, add B♭ to make C-E-G-B♭. Its common symbol is simply C7. The name major-minor seventh explains the construction: the triad is major, while the seventh is minor.

Download recording

Cmaj7 and C7 differ by only one note: B becomes B♭. Once again, changing one note changes the character of the entire harmony.

Why Is It Called Dominant Seventh?

The name comes from one of the chord’s most important roles in tonal harmony. In F major, C is the dominant, so C7 functions as V7. Its chord members have strong tendencies toward resolution, and the tritone E-B♭ helps create that pull.

In F major, E is the leading tone and typically moves up to F. The chordal seventh, B♭, typically moves down to A. Both notes move toward tones of the tonic chord.

Listen: C7 resolving to F major

Download recording

This motion creates the arrival from V7 to I: the dominant seventh creates tension and the tonic provides release. C7 also appears in blues, jazz, secondary-dominant progressions, and other contexts. The name identifies a quality, while the chord’s function depends on the music.

Minor Seventh Chords

A minor seventh chord adds a minor seventh to a minor triad: C-E♭-G plus B♭ gives C-E♭-G-B♭. Common symbols are Cm7, Cmin7, and C-7. The added seventh enriches the triad, often sounding smooth while remaining harmonically active. Minor sevenths occur in classical, jazz, popular, and other traditions; their function depends on context.

Half-Diminished Seventh Chords

A half-diminished seventh chord combines a diminished triad with a minor seventh above the root. Start with C diminished, C-E♭-G♭, and add B♭. The result is C-E♭-G♭-B♭, commonly written Cø7 or Cm7♭5. The second symbol describes a minor seventh chord with a lowered fifth.

Listen: C minor seventh compared with C half-diminished seventh

Download recording

Only the fifth changes from C minor seventh: G becomes G♭, altering the chord’s character. Half-diminished seventh chords are important in tonal and jazz harmony; we will encounter them in leading-tone harmony and minor-key progressions.

Fully Diminished Seventh Chords

A fully diminished seventh chord combines a diminished triad with a diminished seventh above the root. Add B double-flat, B♭♭, to C-E♭-G♭ to make C-E♭-G♭-B♭♭. Its common symbol is C°7.

Listen: C half-diminished seventh compared with C fully diminished seventh

Download recording

Only the seventh changes: B♭ in the half-diminished chord becomes B♭♭ in the fully diminished chord. That one alteration creates a different seventh-chord quality.

Why B Double-Flat?

On an equal-tempered piano, B♭♭ and A sound the same, but their theoretical meanings differ. C to A is a sixth; C to B♭♭ is a diminished seventh. Because this is a seventh chord built in thirds, we spell the final chord member as B♭♭ rather than A.

The spelling C-E♭-G♭-B♭♭ shows three stacked minor thirds connecting four notes. This structure is a defining feature of the fully diminished seventh chord. Its unusual symmetry also helps it connect distant harmonies, making it especially useful in chromatic harmony and modulation.

Seventh-Chord Inversions

Triads have three distinct notes and three possible bass positions. Seventh chords have four distinct notes and four possible bass positions. The chord’s root and quality stay the same; its inversion changes according to the chord member in the bass. Here are the four positions of G7:

Every arrangement is still G7. Changing the bass can nevertheless have a strong effect on how the chord connects to the harmonies around it.

Identifying Chords Outside Root Position

The bass is not necessarily the root. Rearrange E-G-C into stacked thirds, C-E-G, and the structure becomes clear: it is C major. Because E, the third, is in the bass, the chord is in first inversion.

The same process works for seventh chords. Rearrange B-D-F-G into G-B-D-F to identify G7; B in the bass then tells you it is first inversion. Ask two separate questions: “What chord is this?” identifies the root and quality, while “Which chord member is in the bass?” identifies the inversion.

Harmony and Musical Gravity

Chords feel different partly because they combine intervals into larger structures. Major and minor triads contain different thirds above their roots, while augmented and diminished triads alter the fifth. Seventh chords add another chord member and increase the possibilities for tension and resolution.

The deeper answer is that chords exist in musical contexts. A major triad may feel like home in one passage and need to resolve in another. A dominant seventh often pulls toward tonic because of the ways its notes tend to move, while a fully diminished seventh can create instability that leads toward several possible destinations.

Harmony therefore involves motion, expectation, and musical gravity as well as chord spelling. Having learned how chords are constructed, we can now explore how they relate to one another, how their notes move, and how composers organize them into convincing progressions.

A Better Way to Think About Harmony

Four triad types and five seventh-chord types may seem like a lot, but each idea builds on something you already understand. Intervals lead to stacked thirds, different sizes of thirds create triad qualities, an additional third creates a seventh chord, and a change of bass creates an inversion.

Understanding these relationships is more useful than memorizing isolated facts. If you understand intervals, you can construct triads; if you understand triads, you can construct seventh chords. Knowing how chords are built prepares you to understand how they move and why one progression may sound more elegant than another.

Summary

A seventh chord has four distinct chord members: root, third, fifth, and seventh. The five common qualities studied here are major seventh, dominant seventh, minor seventh, half-diminished seventh, and fully diminished seventh. Each is built from a triad and a seventh measured above the root.

Seventh chords have four bass positions: root position, first inversion, second inversion, and third inversion. The root identifies the chord, while the chord member in the bass determines its inversion. These structures are combinations of intervals that create harmonic colors, degrees of stability, and possibilities for musical motion.
